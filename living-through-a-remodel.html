Living Through a Remodel: 12 Practical Tips

Twenty-five years of watching families live in the middle of construction. The households that come through it well are not the patient ones — they're the ones who planned the inconvenience instead of absorbing it.

By Philip Howard, Owner & General Contractor · CA Lic. #836369 Published August 13, 2026 9 min read
Get a Free Estimate → ☎ (707) 578-6565

Last updated August 13, 2026. Figures reflect real project pricing across Sonoma, Napa, Marin and Solano counties and are reviewed each season.

Key Takeaways

  • Decide whether to stay or move out before work starts. Moving out mid-project is expensive and disruptive; deciding early is neither.
  • Set up a temporary kitchen properly. A microwave on a folding table is how a 10-week remodel becomes unbearable.
  • Dust is the thing everyone underestimates. Proper containment and negative air are worth insisting on.
  • Agree communication rules up front — a weekly walkthrough and one point of contact prevents most friction.
  • The final inspection is not a quality review of your finishes. It confirms code compliance. Your punch list is a separate process.

Should you stay or move out?

ProjectUsually livable?
Single bathroom remodel (second bath available)Yes
Kitchen remodelYes, with a real temporary kitchen
Ground-floor additionUsually yes
Second-story additionOften no — roof comes off
Whole-house remodelNo
Only bathroom in the houseNo
Floor refinishing throughoutNo — fumes and cure time

Staying saves real money — a rental in Sonoma or Napa County runs $3,500–$7,000 a month, plus moving and storage. But factor in what you spend eating out with no kitchen, and the honest question you should ask is whether your household can tolerate ten weeks of noise, dust and strangers in the house.

The one thing that reliably ruins it: deciding to stay, discovering in week three that you cannot, and moving out then. You pay for both, on short notice, in a tight rental market. Make the call before demolition.

Twelve things that actually help

1. Build a real temporary kitchen

Not a microwave on a box. Set up: refrigerator, microwave, toaster oven or induction hotplate, kettle, a folding table for prep, a wash station (laundry sink, bathroom, or outdoor spigot with a basin), and a bin for dishes. Locate it away from the work zone. Ten weeks is a long time to live badly.

2. Insist on proper dust containment

Plastic sheeting with zippered doors, not a taped-up bedsheet. Floor protection on every path the crew walks. An air scrubber with HEPA filtration in the work zone, running under negative pressure so air flows into the work area rather than out into your living space. Ask whether this is included — on a good job it is standard.

3. Seal your HVAC

Cover return and supply registers in the work area. Running your system through an active demolition zone distributes fine dust through the entire house and into the equipment.

4. Pack up the work zone completely

Everything. Contents of cabinets, wall art in adjacent rooms, anything on shelves that shares a wall. Vibration travels further than people expect.

5. Plan for pets

Open doors all day, loud tools, and unfamiliar people is a bad combination for most animals. Set up a room the crew has no reason to enter, with a sign on the door, or arrange daycare on the loudest days. Tell your contractor which animals are in the house.

6. Agree working hours in writing

Most jurisdictions restrict construction noise — commonly 7am to 6pm weekdays with limits on weekends. Agree what works for your household and put it in the contract.

7. If you work from home, say so early

A good contractor can often schedule the loudest work — demolition, concrete cutting, framing — into blocks you plan around. But only if they know. Identify your critical call times up front.

8. One point of contact, each way

One person in your household speaks for the project, one person on the crew speaks for the build. Decisions relayed through whoever happens to be standing in the kitchen is how errors happen.

9. Book a standing weekly walkthrough

Thirty minutes at the same time each week to review progress, upcoming decisions and anything discovered. This single habit prevents more disputes than any contract clause.

10. Keep a running decision log

A shared note with every selection and approval, dated. Memory diverges over ten weeks; a log does not.

11. Handle change orders in writing, always

Cost and schedule impact, approved before work proceeds. No verbal approvals, however small it seems in the moment.

12. Plan the last two weeks

Finish work is slow, quiet and feels interminable after the dramatic early progress. Knowing that in advance makes it far easier to sit through.

What happens at the final inspection

Homeowners often expect the final inspection to be a quality review. It is not. The inspector confirms the work complies with code — not that your tile is straight or your paint is even. Those are punch-list items between you and your contractor.

What the inspector checks

  • All prior inspections signed off in sequence
  • Electrical: GFCI and AFCI protection, panel labelling, working outlets and fixtures
  • Plumbing: fixtures functioning, no leaks, correct venting, water heater strapping
  • Mechanical: ventilation ducted correctly, combustion air adequate
  • Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms in required locations, interconnected where required
  • Egress: window sizes in bedrooms, door swings, stair and guardrail dimensions
  • Insulation and Title 24 documentation
  • Handrails, guardrails and stair geometry
  • In WUI zones: Chapter 7A compliance — vents, roof assembly, exterior materials
  • Address numbers visible from the street

If it fails

A failed final is common and not a crisis. The inspector issues a correction notice, your contractor fixes the items, and a re-inspection is scheduled — usually within a few days. What matters is that the permit is finalled, because an open permit follows the property and will surface at resale.

Confirm your permit is closed. Ask your contractor for the signed final and check with the building department. Open permits are one of the most common unpleasant surprises during escrow, sometimes years later.

The punch list, and getting to done

The punch list is your itemised record of everything remaining: touch-ups, adjustments, missing hardware, doors that stick. Walk the project in daylight with your contractor and write it down together.

  • Walk in good light and look deliberately — along walls at a low angle for drywall, at grout lines, at door and drawer alignment.
  • Operate everything. Every door, window, drawer, switch, faucet and appliance.
  • Be specific. "Paint touch-up on the north wall of the hall, left of the light switch," not "paint needs work."
  • Agree a completion date for punch items rather than leaving it open.
  • Hold a final payment until punch items are genuinely complete — this is normal and reasonable.
  • Collect your closeout package: signed final permit, warranties, appliance manuals, paint colours and product specifications, and unconditional lien releases from all subcontractors and suppliers.

That last item matters more than it sounds. Paint colours and product specs are what let you touch up a wall or match a tile in five years without guessing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I live in my house during a kitchen remodel?

Most households can stay during a kitchen remodel provided they set up a genuine temporary kitchen with a refrigerator, microwave, hotplate or toaster oven, prep surface and a wash station away from the work zone. Expect 8 to 16 weeks without a functioning kitchen. Whole-house remodels and projects that remove the only bathroom generally require moving out.

How do I keep construction dust out of the rest of my house?

Insist on plastic containment with zippered doors rather than taped sheeting, floor protection along every path the crew walks, and a HEPA air scrubber running under negative pressure so air flows into the work zone rather than out. Also cover HVAC registers in the work area, since running your system through an active demolition zone spreads fine dust through the whole house.

What does a building inspector check at a final inspection?

The inspector verifies code compliance, not finish quality. They confirm prior inspections were signed off, check GFCI and AFCI protection, panel labelling, plumbing function and venting, water heater strapping, mechanical ventilation, smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, egress window sizes, stair and guardrail dimensions, insulation and Title 24 documentation, and in WUI zones, Chapter 7A compliance.

What if my project fails its final inspection?

A failed final inspection is common and easily resolved. The inspector issues a correction notice listing the items, your contractor completes the corrections, and a re-inspection is scheduled, usually within a few days. What matters is that the permit is ultimately finalled, because an open permit stays attached to the property and will surface at resale.

What should be in a punch list?

A punch list itemises everything remaining at the end of a project: paint touch-ups, misaligned doors or drawers, missing hardware, grout gaps, sticking windows and similar items. Walk the project in daylight with your contractor and record each item specifically, including its location, then agree a completion date. Holding a final payment until punch items are complete is normal practice.
