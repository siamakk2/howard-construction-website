Permit Guide: Sonoma & Napa County Home Construction

What actually requires a permit, what it costs, how long each jurisdiction takes, and why unpermitted work is far more expensive to inherit than to avoid.

By Philip Howard, Owner & General Contractor · CA Lic. #836369 Published August 13, 2026 11 min read
Get a Free Estimate → ☎ (707) 578-6565

Last updated August 13, 2026. Figures reflect real project pricing across Sonoma, Napa, Marin and Solano counties and are reviewed each season.

Key Takeaways

  • Assume you need a permit for anything structural, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, or that adds conditioned space. The exemptions are narrower than most homeowners expect.
  • Permit and plan check costs run $600–$3,500 for a straightforward remodel and $6,000–$25,000 for an addition, plus impact fees on new dwellings.
  • Review timelines run 2–6 weeks for simple work and 6–16 weeks for additions. Incomplete submittals are the number one cause of delay.
  • Each jurisdiction differs: the City of Santa Rosa, City of Napa, City of Calistoga and unincorporated county all run separate fee schedules and processes.
  • Unpermitted work surfaces at resale, can void insurance claims, and often has to be opened up for inspection — costing far more to correct than the permit would have cost.

The trap in kitchen and bathroom remodels: homeowners often assume a "cosmetic" remodel is exempt. But if you add a circuit for a microwave, move a sink even a foot, or replace a fan with one that vents outside, you have crossed into permit territory. Most full kitchen and bathroom remodels require a permit.

Building permit fees are generally calculated from the valuation of the work. On top of the permit itself, larger projects may carry school impact fees, traffic and park fees, water and sewer connection charges, and plan check fees billed separately.

Engineering and reports

  • Structural engineering — $2,000–$12,000 depending on complexity
  • Soils report — $2,500–$6,000, commonly required on hillside or new foundations
  • Title 24 energy compliance — $400–$1,500
  • Survey — $1,500–$5,000 where setbacks are tight

Who reviews your project?

The single most important question is whether your property is inside a city boundary or in unincorporated county. They are different agencies with different fees, standards and timelines — and a Santa Rosa mailing address does not necessarily mean you are inside the City of Santa Rosa.

Additional reviews that catch people out

  • Fire district review — access width, turnarounds, hydrant distance and defensible space on rural parcels
  • Septic / Environmental Health — triggered by adding a bedroom
  • Well permits and water availability analysis
  • Design or historic review in designated districts
  • Floodplain review near the Napa River and Laguna de Santa Rosa
  • Tree removal permits — protected oaks in much of both counties

How the process actually runs

  1. Design and construction documents. Complete drawings, structural calculations, Title 24 compliance.
  2. Submittal. Plans, forms and fees go in. Many jurisdictions now accept electronic submittal.
  3. Plan check. Building, and often planning, fire, and engineering review in parallel.
  4. Corrections. Comments come back; the designer revises and resubmits. Expect at least one cycle; each adds 2–6 weeks.
  5. Issuance. Fees paid, permit issued, work may begin.
  6. Inspections. At defined stages — foundation, framing, rough MEP, insulation, drywall, final.
  7. Final sign-off. Certificate of occupancy where applicable.

The single biggest cause of permit delay is an incomplete submittal. Not a hostile plan checker — a drawing set missing a detail, a calculation, or a form. Time spent making the submittal complete is repaid several times over in review weeks saved.

Inspections: what to expect

Inspections are scheduled by your contractor and must be passed before the work is covered. A failed inspection is not a crisis; it means a correction and a re-inspection. What is a genuine problem is covering work before it has been inspected, because it then has to be opened back up.

What unpermitted work costs you later

The reason to permit is not fear of enforcement. It is that unpermitted work becomes your problem at the worst possible moment.

  • At resale. California requires disclosure of known unpermitted work. Buyers routinely demand a price reduction, legalization, or removal. Appraisers may not count unpermitted square footage toward value at all — so a $200,000 unpermitted addition can appraise at nothing.
  • Insurance. Carriers can deny a claim arising from unpermitted work. In a region with genuine wildfire exposure, that is a serious risk to carry.
  • Legalizing after the fact. Permits can usually be obtained retroactively, but the work has to be verified — which frequently means opening walls, exposing framing and electrical, and correcting anything not to code. Costs commonly run 2–4 times what permitting would have cost originally.
  • Refinancing. Lenders may refuse to count unpermitted space, affecting appraised value.
  • Safety. Inspections exist because uninspected electrical and structural work fails in ways that are not visible until they matter.

If you have inherited unpermitted work

It is fixable. The usual path is a records search at the building department to establish what was permitted, an assessment of what exists, then a retroactive permit application with drawings of the as-built condition. Some jurisdictions run amnesty programs. It is nearly always cheaper to resolve it deliberately than to have it discovered during escrow.

Frequently Asked Questions

What requires a building permit in Sonoma County?

Sonoma County requires a permit for structural changes, new or relocated electrical circuits, plumbing relocation, water heater and HVAC replacement, re-roofing, new or resized windows, decks over 30 inches high or 200 square feet, pools, additions, ADUs and garage conversions. Purely cosmetic work such as painting, flooring, or countertop replacement generally does not require one.

How long does a building permit take in Napa County?

Plan review typically takes 3 to 8 weeks inside the City of Napa and 6 to 16 weeks in unincorporated Napa County. Additions and new construction sit at the longer end, and at least one correction cycle is normal, adding another 2 to 6 weeks. Incomplete submittals are the most common cause of extended timelines.

What happens if I sell a house with unpermitted work in California?

California requires sellers to disclose known unpermitted work. Buyers commonly respond by requesting a price reduction, retroactive permitting, or removal of the work. Appraisers often exclude unpermitted square footage from valuation entirely, and lenders may decline to count it, which can affect both sale price and the buyer's financing.

Can I get a permit for work that was already done?

Yes, retroactive permits are generally available, but the completed work has to be verified against current code. That usually means opening walls to expose framing, electrical and plumbing for inspection, then correcting anything non-compliant. The total cost commonly runs two to four times what permitting the work correctly at the time would have cost.

Do I need a permit to replace a water heater in California?

Yes. Water heater replacement requires a plumbing permit in virtually every California jurisdiction, because it involves gas or electrical connections, seismic strapping, venting and expansion control. The permit is inexpensive, typically $150 to $450, and the inspection confirms the seismic strapping and venting are correct.

Should my contractor or I pull the permit?

Your licensed contractor should pull the permit. An owner-builder permit shifts legal responsibility for the work onto you, including liability for injuries and for code compliance. If a contractor asks you to pull the permit instead, it frequently indicates they are unlicensed or unwilling to have the work inspected under their own license.
